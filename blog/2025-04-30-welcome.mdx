### Welcome to JJK.ENGINEER

Welcome to jjk.engineer, a place where I can express my cerebral meanderings, snark, musings, and have a little fun. Now what?

{/* truncate */}

### Intro

Hi, I'm Justin. I'm on a mission.

### The mission

1. To write.
2. To code.

#### Writing

It's been said, I'm sure, but don't know by whom, that writing is a form of thinking. I like thinking, you'd think I'd like writing. And I do, but finding a way to do it is a struggle. I've explored all the different writing forms. Word, Google Docs, Notion, pen and paper, all of which were to some degree or another, satisfying, in a way, but by keeping them relatively private, there was never a real push to do it well. That lack of development always seemed to leave something to be desired. Eventually, I landed on Obsidian, which I still use. It's still all private, but I came to love writing with Markdown.

This new-found interest in writing with Markdown, dovetailing with my desire to get it out to the public to encourage me to write better, thereby thinking deeper. So I started tinkering with blogs online. I turned to WordPress. If you just want to write, it's fine. But I've always itched for just a bit more than writing alone, and the clunkiness I felt with WordPress encouraged me to dive deeper into websites and what not.

#### Coding

A long time ago, in a high school far far away I took a class on computer programming. It sparked a neverending curiousity to tinker. In that course, I remeber lerning Turbo Pascal in the first half of the year and the second we did Visual Basic. It was rather depressing though to think that the only way to code like this was gaining access to VisualStudio, which, at the time, was cost prohibitive (probably still is). Years later, after having abandoned any of this coding stuff on account of the perceived barrier to entry, I went to college for engineering and learned a couple more languages; assembly, C++, Matlab, PLCs, etc. They were all interesting, but I still didn't know of a way to code for free, or at the very least, very low cost, as I was a poor college student.

My first job was in industrial automation and I got to tinker quite a bit with coding from a PLC standpoint, it was much more ladder logic and routines than raw coding in the way we might visualize, but I was exposed to an HMI platform called Inductive Automation which was a browser-based HMI platform where I built the operator interface for a small plant in SETX along with the PLC control system it interacted with. Inductive Automation, being browser based, introduced me to HTML, as I could write raw HTML to create the objects on the screen if the drag and drop stuff didn't suit my fancy, and much didn't. I don't recall if I ever branched out from the raw HTML.

Sometime thereafter, I was trying to start a business with a college friend and we felt like we needed a website, since it was, by this time, _a thing_, so I took it upon myself to try and create one with HTML, since I knew of it after my experience with Inductive Automation. This kept the interest alive, but the site I created was, how do I say it, dismal. Eventually, we spooled up a legit website using Wix. It felt constraining, the drag-and-drop blocks plus the config. It felt boring. We also had had a bit of experience in the corporate world and felt the need to systematize our business and were strained by the lack of tooling for doing so, and the _best_ options were, yet again, cost prohibitive (and I'm not even sure they were the best options, just giant corporations with one-size-fits-most applications you could pay exorbitant fees to config for your organization to use a tiny fraction of).

We were well into the internet age by this time and I just knew there had to be a way to solve these problems, when, per chance, I met a young college-age coder from Nepal working at a gas station by my house. We hired him as an intern to build us a website to tool us up with a custom-built website for our company that we had done our best to map out in what eventually became known as our company's "DNA".

Then COVID hit. Then hurricane Laura hit and the whole thing fell apart.

During the next chapter in my life, I watched the organization I worked for fighting with the hell of endless spreadsheets, most of which were derived from applications so clunky and useless we just exported the data from the application to Excel and worked with it that way until someone _had_ to put it back in the application. I watched this organization build one PowerApp and PowerBI visualization after another that never seemed to work right or get the UX even close to right or even usable. And then they replaced the one main clunky, useless app with yet another clunky, useless app (with better makup on). So I got fed up with it and build my own little tool with the encouragement of one of my coleagues who happened to be a retired software developer.

I built this website using [Docusaurus](https://docusaurus.io/).

The website is hosted on [GitHub](https://github.com) via GitHub Pages.

Docusaurus helped me scaffold up a usable blog/docs website that does _not_ prelcude me from tinkering with the coding stuff I also want to get into. GitHub and GitHub Pages allows me to host the site for free. The only thing I actually pay for is the custom domain name.

Docusaurus is a slight deviation from my original intent of building every single thing from scratch using [Angular](https://angular.dev/) which I do like, and play with for other needs. Docusaurus is built on [React](https://react.dev/) which is not completely foreign to me, but something new.

Over time you will see new things coming out on the site that is the result of my tinkering with code, so stand by. Otherwise, you will see my writing coming out quicker because I'm not building any of that for myself.

I started using [Obsidian](https://obsidian.md/) a long time ago and it taught me to use [Markdown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markdown) and I learned to love it. I wanted a way to write my blog posts in Markdown format and right in my [IDE](https://vscode.dev/).

But long before Obsidian, I had built a website on Wordpress, but I really didn't like it, customizing it didn't allow me to flex my coding muscles the way I wanted to. This allows me to.
